## Grammar Spotlight: Modal Perfect

The modal perfect (*modal verb* + *have* + past participle) lets us look back at the past and comment on it: guessing what happened, judging what should have happened, or imagining what didn't. The modal chooses the attitude — *must have* for near-certainty, *might have* or *could have* for possibility, *should have* for criticism or regret.

- Certain: The room **must have been** noisy that summer.
- Possible: They **could have chosen** a duller name for the field.
- Regret: They **should have promised** less.

## The Two Men Who Changed the Subject

In October 1950 the philosophy journal *Mind* published a paper by the mathematician Alan Turing that opened with a question anyone can understand: "Can machines think?" Turing then did something unexpected. Instead of answering, he declared the question too vague to be worth discussing, and replaced it with a game.

In his imitation game, a person sits at a keyboard and exchanges written messages with two hidden participants — one human, one machine — and has to work out which is which. Turing's move was to swap an unanswerable question about inner life for something you can actually observe: behaviour. He also made a prediction. By the year 2000, he wrote, a computer would play the game so well that an average interrogator would have "not more than a 70-percent chance" of identifying it correctly after five minutes.

Five years later, four scientists made the same move in a different way. On 31 August 1955, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon sent a proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation. "We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College" — the first time anyone had written that phrase down. The work would proceed, they wrote, "on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

They asked for $13,500. Reading it now, you might think they must have been joking about the timescale: eight weeks to make machines use language, form concepts and improve themselves. They were not.

The workshop went ahead. It was supposed to produce a report. Decades later, McCarthy annotated his own archived copy of the proposal with four words: "There was no report."

## Key Vocabulary

- **to sidestep** — to avoid dealing with something directly. *He sidestepped the question rather than admit he had no answer.*
- **conjecture** — an idea assumed to be true without proof, used as a starting point. *The whole project rested on a single conjecture.*
- **interrogator** — someone whose role is to question another person. *The interrogator could not see either participant.*
- **to annotate** — to add notes or comments to a document. *He annotated the proposal years after writing it.*
- **timescale** — the period of time something is expected to take. *Their timescale now looks wildly optimistic.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Turing decided that "Can machines think?" was too vague to answer, and replaced it with a test of behaviour. Was that a clever solution or an evasion?
2. If you had to design a test today for whether a machine can think, what would you actually measure?
3. The Dartmouth group booked eight weeks to solve problems we still haven't solved. Why do you think experts so often underestimate difficulty?
4. Does it matter whether a machine really understands something, as long as it behaves as though it does?

## Quick Recap

In 1950 Turing replaced the question "Can machines think?" with an observable test — the imitation game — and predicted machines would pass it by 2000. In 1955 four scientists named the field "artificial intelligence" in a funding proposal and gave themselves one summer to make serious progress. Both moments founded the field by confidently framing a problem rather than solving it.
