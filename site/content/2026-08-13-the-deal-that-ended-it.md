## Grammar Spotlight: Future in the Past

To describe something that lay in the future *at some past moment* — especially when it did not happen as expected — English uses *was/were going to*, *would*, and *was/were to*. It is the natural grammar of broken promises and unintended outcomes.

- **What was going to be** a brief intervention lasted twenty-one years.
- The generals said the measures **would be** temporary.
- The campaign **was to become** the largest in the country's history.

## What Was Going to Be Temporary

When the military removed President João Goulart in 1964, the intervention was presented as a correction: a short period of order, after which normal politics would resume. It was going to be brief. It lasted twenty-one years.

The clearest measure of how far it travelled from that promise is Institutional Act Number Five, signed on 13 December 1968. AI-5 closed congress, imposed censorship on the press and the arts, stripped citizens of political rights, and removed habeas corpus for political offences. The years between 1968 and 1974 are generally identified as the period of harshest repression.

What that meant in practice was documented decades later. The National Truth Commission, which worked for two years and seven months and delivered its report on 10 December 2014, ran to more than four thousand pages across three volumes. It named 434 people killed or disappeared, and concluded that illegal detention, torture, sexual violence, execution and the concealment of bodies had not been excesses by individuals but the result of state policy — amounting, in its assessment, to crimes against humanity.

The ending is where this series' pattern shows most uncomfortably. In 1979 an Amnesty Law was passed covering both those who had resisted the regime and those who had run its machinery of repression. In 1984 the *Diretas Já* campaign filled the streets: on 16 April around 1.5 million people gathered in São Paulo, the largest political demonstration the country had seen. They were demanding the right to elect the next president directly. The constitutional amendment that would have given them that fell twenty-two votes short of the two-thirds majority it needed.

So the transition happened another way. On 15 January 1985 an electoral college — not the public — chose Tancredo Neves as president, by 480 votes to 180. He died on 21 April, before taking office, and his vice-president José Sarney took over.

Democracy returned. It returned through an indirect vote, after the direct-election amendment had failed, under an amnesty that protected the outgoing regime. A million and a half people had asked for something else.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to strip** (someone of something) — to take away formally. *Citizens were stripped of political rights.*
- **concealment** — the deliberate hiding of something. *The report described the concealment of bodies.*
- **amnesty** — an official pardon, often for political offences. *The 1979 amnesty covered both sides.*
- **to fall short** — to fail to reach a required level. *The amendment fell twenty-two votes short.*
- **electoral college** — a body that elects on the public's behalf. *An electoral college chose the president.*

## Discussion Questions

1. The 1979 amnesty protected people on both sides. Was that a fair price for a peaceful transition, or too high a one?
2. A million and a half people demonstrated and the amendment still failed. What does that say about how change actually happens?
3. The Truth Commission reported thirty years after the events. Is there a right moment for a country to examine its own past?
4. This period is within living memory for many Brazilian families. How is it talked about in yours?

## Quick Recap

The 1964 coup was announced as a brief correction and lasted twenty-one years, hardening with AI-5 in 1968. The 2014 Truth Commission named 434 dead or disappeared and found that torture and execution had been state policy. Despite the 1.5-million-strong Diretas Já rally in 1984, the direct-election amendment failed, and democracy returned in 1985 through an electoral college, under an amnesty protecting the outgoing regime.
