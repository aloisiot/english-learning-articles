## Grammar Spotlight: Passive Voice

We use the passive voice when the action matters more than who performs it, or when the doer is unknown, obvious, or unimportant. It's formed with a form of *be* + past participle, and the doer can be added with *by* if it's worth mentioning.

- Active: Confucius **taught** that rituals shape character.
- Passive: It **was taught** that rituals shape character.
- Active: Family relationships **train** a person's virtue over a lifetime.
- Passive: A person's virtue **is trained** by family relationships over a lifetime.

## The Self Nobody Builds Alone

Ask most people in the West what makes them who they are, and they will likely point somewhere inside: a personality, a set of private beliefs, a "true self" that exists whether or not anyone else is around. Confucius, writing in China around the fifth century BCE, would not have recognised the question.

In Confucian thought, the self is not something you already have and later decorate with relationships. It is built by them. A person is understood as a son or daughter, a neighbour, a friend, a ruler or a subject — and it is through these roles, not despite them, that character is formed. Two ideas carry most of the weight here. Ren (仁), often translated as "humaneness" or "benevolence," is described as the inner attitude of warmth and care from which good conduct flows. Li (禮) — ritual, etiquette, the accumulated forms of respectful behaviour — is how that inner attitude is expressed outwardly, in everything from a greeting to a funeral rite.

The two are meant to work together. Li without ren is empty performance — the right bow, the wrong heart. Ren without li has no way to be recognised or passed on; it stays a private feeling that never becomes a shared culture. Li has even been compared to a grammar: a structure that lets an inner disposition be communicated, generation after generation, without having to be reinvented by each new person from nothing.

This is why the Confucian self is called relational rather than individual. It isn't smaller for being shaped by others — family, teachers, community, even strangers passed on the street are treated as part of the material the self is made from, not as walls around it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **benevolence** — genuine goodwill and care toward others. *His benevolence was obvious in how patiently he listened.*
- **ritual** — a fixed, formal way of carrying out an action, often carrying symbolic meaning. *The tea ceremony is a ritual passed down for centuries.*
- **conduct** — the way a person behaves, especially judged by a standard. *Her conduct at the meeting impressed everyone.*
- **disposition** — a person's natural tendency to think or act in a certain way. *He has a calm disposition, even under pressure.*
- **to shape** (someone/something) — to influence how something develops or turns out. *Her childhood was shaped by frequent moves.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Do you think your own sense of self is shaped more by your relationships or by something more private and internal? Can you separate the two?
2. Confucius saw ritual — even small things like greetings — as important for building character. Do you think small daily habits shape who we become?
3. Is there a risk in defining a person mainly through their roles (parent, employee, neighbour) rather than as an individual? What might be lost?
4. Can you think of a "ritual" in your own culture that expresses care without needing words?

## Quick Recap

Confucian philosophy treats the self as something built through relationships and ritual, not a fixed private thing. Ren (inner benevolence) and li (its outward, ritual expression) work together: one without the other is either empty performance or a feeling with no way to be shared.
