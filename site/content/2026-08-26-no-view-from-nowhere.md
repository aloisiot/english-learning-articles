## Grammar Spotlight: Result and Purpose Clauses

Result clauses say what happened *because* something was extreme: *so + adjective + that*, or *such + a/an + noun + that*. Purpose clauses say what something was *for*: *so that*, *in order to*, *so as to*. The pair is easy to confuse, because both often use *so*.

- Result: The claim was **so radical that** his contemporaries could not use it.
- Result: It was **such a direct attack that** no reply was possible.
- Purpose: He wrote in fragments **so that** no system could be built from them.

## Taking Away the Ladder

Kant had left one thing standing behind the world of appearances: the thing in itself, real but unknowable. Nietzsche's reply was that this is not a modest admission of limits. It is an empty phrase.

The argument is short. Strip away every property you could know through experience — colour, extension, position, relation to anything else — and ask what is left over to be the thing. Nothing is left. A propertyless thing is not a mystery; it is a contradiction dressed up as humility. And Kant's whole critical project, Nietzsche noted with some enjoyment, ended up in the business of legislation rather than criticism. It produced a moral law, the categorical imperative, and never once asked the harder question: what is the *value* of our moral ideals, or of knowledge itself? The critique stopped precisely where it should have started.

Against Kant's grounding of morality in reason, with the instincts and desires firmly set aside, Nietzsche put those instincts back at the centre — will to power in place of duty. This is where careless reading does the most damage, so it is worth being plain: this is a claim about what actually drives human beings and their values, not a political programme, whatever later people made of it.

What replaces the thing in itself is perspectivism: there is no absolute standpoint, no God's-eye view from which everything can be surveyed at once. Every account of the world is an account *from* somewhere, shaped by the interests and needs of whoever is giving it. Which takes away the ladder the whole tradition had been climbing — Plato's Forms, Descartes' certainty, Kant's a priori structure were all attempts to reach exactly such a view.

The famous line belongs here. "God is dead" appears in *The Gay Science* of 1882, and it is not a cheer. Nietzsche presents it as a challenge to complacency: he thought people had already stopped believing without noticing what they had been relying on. The condemnation is aimed at absolute perspectives and values generally, and the point is to turn attention back to the world we actually live in, away from what he called escapist, pain-relieving, heavenly otherworlds — including the philosophical ones.

Which is where this series stops. Two and a half thousand years after Socrates started asking people what they really knew, the answer arrived at is that the question had a false assumption buried in it: that there is a stable order behind appearances, and a place to stand from which anyone could see it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **complacency** — self-satisfaction that stops you noticing problems. *He wrote to attack his readers' complacency, not to comfort them.*
- **to survey** — to look at the whole of something. *There is no standpoint from which we could survey everything at once.*
- **standpoint** — the position from which you look at something. *Every account comes from a particular standpoint.*
- **escapist** — offering a way of avoiding reality. *He dismissed such beliefs as escapist.*
- **to dress up as** — to present something as if it were something better. *A contradiction dressed up as humility is still a contradiction.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Nietzsche says there is no neutral standpoint — every account comes from somewhere. Does that make disagreement impossible to settle, or just harder?
2. He accused Kant of writing rules instead of asking whether the rules were worth anything. When have you seen that happen in real life?
3. "God is dead" was meant as a warning, not a celebration. Why do you think it is so often quoted as the opposite?
4. After six thinkers and two thousand years, the series ends with the question dissolved rather than answered. Is that a failure?

## Quick Recap

Nietzsche rejected Kant's thing in itself as an incoherent idea — a thing with no knowable properties is nothing at all — and accused Kant's critique of legislating morality instead of questioning its value. In place of duty he put instinct and will to power, and in place of an absolute standpoint, perspectivism: every view is a view from somewhere. That removes the ground the whole tradition from Plato onwards had been standing on.
