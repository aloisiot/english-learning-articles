## Grammar Spotlight: *used to* and *would*

Both describe the past as something that is over. ***Used to*** works for repeated actions **and** for states that are no longer true. ***Would*** works only for repeated actions, and usually needs the time frame established first. Neither is used for a single completed event.

- State: Power **used to** rotate between two states. (not *would rotate*, as a first mention)
- Repeated action: Once he had settled in, he **would speak** to workers directly on the radio.
- Both: Governors **used to** control their own votes, and each election **would confirm** the arrangement.

## The Man Who Wrote the Rules You Still Work Under

Before 1930, Brazilian politics ran on an arrangement so predictable it had a nickname: *café com leite*, coffee with milk. The presidency used to alternate between São Paulo, which grew the coffee, and Minas Gerais, which produced the milk. Elections would confirm what the state governors had already agreed among themselves.

Getúlio Vargas ended that in the Revolution of 1930, and then stayed for fifteen years. In November 1937, citing a communist threat, he dissolved the arrangement entirely and declared the *Estado Novo* — an openly dictatorial regime with censorship, a secret police and no congress. It lasted until October 1945.

Judging him is harder than that summary suggests, and Brazilians have been arguing about it ever since. In 1943 his government issued the *Consolidação das Leis do Trabalho*, a single labour code covering hours, holidays, dismissal and much else. It is not a historical curiosity: it still structures how Brazilians are employed. Unions were legally recognised, wages rose, and welfare reached growing numbers of workers — but the same laws kept unions under state supervision. Rights were granted from above, and the state kept the machinery that granted them.

His propaganda called him *o pai dos pobres*, the father of the poor. His critics completed the phrase: *mãe dos ricos*, the mother of the rich.

He returned in 1951, this time elected. In August 1954, facing a corruption scandal and pressure from the military to resign, he shot himself. He left a letter blaming foreign and domestic interests for the campaign against him — a document that transformed his reputation within hours. The man who used to be attacked as a dictator would be mourned, almost immediately, as a martyr.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to alternate** — to change repeatedly between two things. *The presidency used to alternate between two states.*
- **to dissolve** — to formally end an assembly or arrangement. *He dissolved congress in 1937.*
- **dismissal** — the act of ending someone's employment. *The code set rules for dismissal.*
- **supervision** — official oversight and control. *Unions operated under state supervision.*
- **martyr** — someone who dies and is then honoured for a cause. *He was mourned as a martyr.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Can a government that removes political freedom still be credited with improving people's lives? How should the two be weighed?
2. The CLT is still in force. Does living under a law change how you judge the person who wrote it?
3. Rights that are granted can also be withdrawn. Does it matter whether people win them or receive them?
4. Vargas is remembered very differently by different Brazilians. Which version did you grow up hearing?

## Quick Recap

Vargas took power in 1930, ending the *café com leite* republic, and ruled as an open dictator under the Estado Novo from 1937 to 1945. His 1943 labour code still shapes Brazilian employment, though it also kept unions under state control. Elected again in 1951, he killed himself in 1954, leaving a letter that rapidly turned him from dictator into martyr.
