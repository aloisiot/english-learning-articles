## Grammar Spotlight: Third and Mixed Conditionals

The **third conditional** describes an imagined past — something that did not happen, and its imagined past result: *If we had built more capacity, we would have avoided the shortage.*

A **mixed conditional** combines a past condition with a present result, which is useful when an old decision still shapes today: *If we had invested in the grid a decade ago, we wouldn't be facing these limits now.*

- Third: If regulators **had anticipated** the demand, they **would have approved** the projects earlier.
- Mixed: If planning **had started** in 2015, the region **wouldn't be** short of power today.

## Article

For most of the past decade, the story of artificial intelligence has been told through chips. Faster processors, bigger models, larger training runs. The assumption was that if we could just manufacture enough hardware, progress would take care of itself.

That assumption is now colliding with something distinctly unglamorous: the electricity grid. Global data centre power demand is expected to rise around twenty-seven percent in 2026, reaching roughly 132 gigawatts, up from 104 the year before. AI-optimised servers alone are projected to account for nearly a third of that consumption, and analysts expect them to overtake conventional servers entirely within a year or two.

The problem is that grids cannot be built as quickly as data centres can. A new facility can be commissioned in a couple of years; a major transmission line can take a decade. Analysts at Gartner have suggested that power shortages could restrict as many as forty percent of AI data centres by 2027. If utilities had begun expanding capacity when the first large language models appeared, the industry would not be running into these walls quite so early.

The strain is unevenly distributed. Ireland's data centres already consume more than twenty percent of the country's electricity. Northern Virginia, parts of Texas, and Phoenix are facing serious expansion costs. Local residents, who did not choose to host this infrastructure, increasingly find themselves in the middle of the debate — and are beginning to ask who exactly is paying for it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **bottleneck** — the point where a process is most restricted. *Manufacturing capacity was the bottleneck, not demand.*
- **to commission** — to bring a facility into operation. *The plant was commissioned two years ahead of schedule.*
- **unglamorous** — dull, lacking excitement or prestige. *Most infrastructure work is unglamorous but essential.*
- **strain** — pressure that risks causing damage or failure. *The heatwave put enormous strain on the grid.*
- **utility** — a company supplying electricity, water, or gas. *Utilities are struggling to forecast demand.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Should communities have a say in whether large data centres are built near them? What would a fair process look like?
2. Is it reasonable to slow down AI development because of energy limits, or should we simply build more power capacity?
3. Who do you think should pay for grid upgrades — the technology companies, the utilities, or the public?
4. Can you think of another technology whose real limitation turned out to be something unexpected?

## Quick Recap

AI's growth is increasingly constrained not by chips but by electricity: data centre power demand is rising sharply while grid expansion takes far longer to deliver. Some regions already face acute strain, raising questions about who bears the cost.
