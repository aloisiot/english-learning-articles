## Grammar Spotlight: Concessive Clauses

A concessive clause concedes a point that runs against your main claim — it says "this is true, and yet". *Although* and *even though* introduce a full clause; *despite* and *in spite of* are followed by a noun or an *-ing* form; *whereas* and *while* set two facts side by side.

- **Although** the system appears to run by itself, people trained it.
- **Despite** winning on one legal question, the company still paid.
- The electricity is measurable, **whereas** the labour is largely invisible.

## The Costs That Were Inputs All Along

A chatbot that declines to produce something violent looks self-governing, as though the machine simply knows better. It does not. It was taught, example by example, by people.

In January 2023 the magazine *TIME* reported that OpenAI had used outsourced workers in Kenya, employed through the firm Sama, to label text drawn from the worst parts of the internet — descriptions of violence, hate speech and abuse — so that ChatGPT could learn to recognise and filter such material. They earned roughly $1.32 to $2 an hour. Although the work was psychologically demanding, support was reported as thin. The invisibility is the point: what looks like an automatic safety feature is a record of what somebody read, repeatedly, for two dollars an hour.

A second bill arrived through the courts. In June 2025 a US judge ruled in a case brought by authors against Anthropic that training a model on lawfully obtained books counted as fair use — but that downloading those books from pirate sites did not. Despite winning the first question, the company settled for $1.5 billion, covering roughly half a million books; final approval came in July 2026, reported as the largest copyright recovery on record. Many similar cases remain unresolved, so the law here is genuinely unsettled rather than decided.

Then there is electricity. Data-centre demand is climbing steeply, whereas the power grids that serve them take far longer to build than the buildings do.

What connects the three is that none of them were accidents. The labelled text, the books and the power were not unfortunate consequences of building these systems. They were the ingredients.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to outsource** — to pay an outside company to do work your organisation would otherwise do. *The moderation work was outsourced to a firm in Nairobi.*
- **to settle** (a case) — to end a legal dispute by agreement rather than judgement. *They settled rather than go to trial.*
- **unsettled** — not yet decided or agreed. *The legal position remains unsettled.*
- **thin** (of support) — barely adequate. *Psychological support was thin.*
- **ingredient** — something required to make a thing, not an optional extra. *Cheap labour was an ingredient, not a side effect.*

## Discussion Questions

1. If a service depends on people being paid two dollars an hour somewhere else, does using it make you responsible in any way?
2. The court separated *training on a book* from *how the book was obtained*. Does that distinction make sense to you?
3. Should writers and artists be paid when their work is used to train a system that may compete with them?
4. Communities near large data centres carry real costs. Who should decide whether one gets built — and who should pay for the power?

## Quick Recap

The systems that appear automatic were assembled from inputs with real costs: Kenyan workers paid around $2 an hour to label disturbing content, half a million books obtained from pirate sites in a case that settled for a record $1.5 billion, and rising electricity demand. These were requirements for building the technology, not accidental side effects.
