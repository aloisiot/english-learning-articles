## Grammar Spotlight: Reduced Relative Clauses

A relative clause can often be shortened by deleting the relative pronoun and the verb *be*. **The objects that are found in nature** becomes **the objects found in nature**. With an active meaning, use the *-ing* form: **the man who is standing there** becomes **the man standing there**. This is everywhere in written English, and it makes dense descriptive sentences much lighter.

- Passive: the properties **which are shared** by all men → the properties **shared** by all men
- Active: a form **which exists** apart from matter → a form **existing** apart from matter
- Stacked: every object **that is made** of matter → every object **made** of matter

## The Argument That Wanted the Explanation Inside the Thing

Aristotle studied at the Academy for about twenty years, which is a long time to spend inside a theory you eventually reject. His objection was not that the Forms were strange. It was that they did not do the job they had been invented to do.

Plato had introduced Forms to explain what particular things have in common. But a Form, Aristotle pointed out, is supposed to be a universal — and yet Plato treats it as a particular thing existing on its own, somewhere else. Universals shared by many things and individual objects standing apart from them are two different kinds of item, and the theory quietly runs them together.

Then comes the argument that gave later philosophers the most trouble, usually called the Third Man. If we need a Form of Man to explain what all individual men have in common, then look at the Form of Man and the individual men together: they have something in common too. Explaining *that* requires a further Form. Which requires another. The explanation never arrives; it just recedes, one step at a time, forever.

Aristotle's alternative keeps the form and moves it. Ask what makes a bronze statue the thing it is, and the answer has two parts: the bronze, and the shape organising it. Neither part exists separately — there is no shape floating free of material, and no lump of matter with no form at all. An ordinary object is matter and form together. The form is *in* the thing, not stored in another world of which the thing is a copy.

The consequence for knowledge is the one that mattered for the next two thousand years. If what makes something what it is sits inside it, then you learn by examining things: dissecting animals, watching what falls, collecting cases and abstracting the general pattern from them. Knowledge starts at the bottom, with particulars, and works upward.

It is worth being careful here. Aristotle did not reject forms, essences, or the idea that things have natural purposes — he kept all three, and his universe is still deeply purposeful. What he rejected was their separation from the world. That distinction gets flattened easily, and flattening it makes him look far more modern than he was.

His framework became the backbone of medieval European learning. Which is exactly why breaking with it, centuries later, meant breaking with everything.

## Key Vocabulary

- **universal** — a property or type shared by many individual things. *Redness is a universal; this red cup is a particular.*
- **to recede** — to move further away. *Each new Form made the explanation recede one more step.*
- **to abstract** — to draw out a general idea from specific cases. *He abstracted the pattern from hundreds of observations.*
- **essence** — what makes a thing the kind of thing it is. *For Aristotle, a thing's essence is in the thing, not outside it.*
- **backbone** — the main support of a structure or system. *His framework was the backbone of medieval learning.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Aristotle spent twenty years learning a theory before rejecting it. Is that a waste of two decades, or the only way to reject something properly?
2. The Third Man argument says the explanation keeps receding. Can you think of a modern explanation that has the same problem?
3. Aristotle says you should study things by examining them closely. That sounds obvious now — why do you think it wasn't obvious then?
4. Is there a subject where you trust careful observation more than expert theory, or the reverse?

## Quick Recap

Aristotle rejected Plato's Forms on the grounds that they confuse universals with particulars, and that the Third Man argument makes the explanation regress endlessly. His alternative puts form inside the object, inseparable from its matter, which makes knowledge a matter of examining things rather than looking past them. He kept essences and purposes — he only refused to locate them elsewhere.
