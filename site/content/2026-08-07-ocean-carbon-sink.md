## Grammar Spotlight: Reported Speech

Reported speech is how we describe what someone said without quoting them word for word. The verb usually shifts one step back into the past, and pronouns and time expressions change to match the new perspective. Notice that reporting verbs such as *say*, *tell*, *warn*, *claim*, and *argue* each carry a slightly different attitude toward the statement.

- Direct: "The ocean **is absorbing** less carbon than before."
- Reported: Researchers said the ocean **was absorbing** less carbon than before.

- Direct: "We **have underestimated** the risk."
- Reported: She admitted they **had underestimated** the risk.

## Article

For as long as humans have been burning fossil fuels, the ocean has been cleaning up after us. Roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide we release ends up dissolved in seawater rather than trapped in the atmosphere. Scientists have long described this as one of the planet's most valuable free services — a service nobody pays for and almost nobody notices.

That service may now be faltering. Researchers reported that in 2023 the ocean absorbed nearly a billion tonnes less carbon dioxide than recent trends had predicted, a drop of around ten percent. They explained that unusually warm water, particularly in the North Atlantic, was largely responsible: warm water simply holds less dissolved gas than cold water does.

A major international assessment released in early 2026 went further. Its authors, drawn from twenty-three countries, warned that we still do not understand the ocean carbon sink well enough to predict how it will behave over the coming decades. One striking finding was that over a twenty-year period, the ocean's sensitivity to rising emissions had declined by roughly fifteen percent — meaning each additional tonne we emit is absorbed slightly less efficiently than the last.

The implication is uncomfortable. Climate models generally assume the ocean will keep absorbing carbon at a predictable rate. If that assumption is wrong, emissions targets calculated on the basis of it may be more generous than they should be. The ocean, it seems, has been doing us an enormous favour — and it is beginning to ask for a break.

## Key Vocabulary

- **carbon sink** — a natural system that absorbs more carbon than it releases. *Forests and oceans are the planet's two largest carbon sinks.*
- **to falter** — to lose strength or momentum. *Support for the policy began to falter after the report was published.*
- **to dissolve** — to become absorbed into a liquid. *Carbon dioxide dissolves into seawater at the surface.*
- **assessment** — a formal evaluation or judgement. *The assessment drew on the work of seventy-two authors.*
- **assumption** — something accepted as true without proof. *The forecast rests on an assumption that may no longer hold.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Do you think most people are aware of how much the ocean does to slow climate change? Why or why not?
2. When scientists say they "do not understand something well enough to predict it," how should governments respond — wait for better data, or act immediately?
3. Are there other "free services" from nature that we take for granted in the same way?
4. How much responsibility do you think ordinary individuals have here, compared to governments and large companies?

## Quick Recap

The ocean absorbs about a quarter of human carbon emissions, but recent research suggests this capacity is weakening — partly because warmer water holds less dissolved gas. Scientists warn that climate models assuming a steady ocean sink may therefore be too optimistic.
