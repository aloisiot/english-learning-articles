## Grammar Spotlight: Inversion for Emphasis

When a sentence begins with a negative or restrictive expression — *never*, *rarely*, *not until*, *only later*, *seldom* — English inverts the subject and the auxiliary verb, as in a question. This is formal, and it adds weight. Note that a verb which has no auxiliary borrows *do*, *does* or *did*.

- Normal: The field had never promised so much so publicly.
- Inverted: **Never had** the field promised so much so publicly.
- Normal: It only became clear later that the report was one man's opinion.
- Inverted: **Only later did** it become clear that the report was one man's opinion.

## How to Talk a Field Into a Collapse

In 1958 the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt published his model of the perceptron, a simple learning machine loosely modelled on neurons. It could be trained to recognise patterns, and both the press and Rosenblatt himself suggested that machines like it would soon see, play chess and even reproduce themselves. Rarely has a modest piece of engineering carried such an enormous public promise.

The correction came in stages. In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published *Perceptrons*, proving mathematically that the simple version could not compute certain basic functions. Then, in July 1972, the British Science Research Council received a 49-page report from Sir James Lighthill, a distinguished mathematician who worked on fluid dynamics — not on artificial intelligence. His verdict was blunt: "In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised." The underlying cause, he argued, was a failure to recognise the "combinatorial explosion" — the way possibilities multiply so fast that a method working on a toy problem collapses on a real one.

Within months, British funding was cut at all but a couple of universities, and American funders followed. A second collapse arrived around 1987, when the market for expensive expert systems and specialised AI computers evaporated.

Only later did the awkward details become widely noticed. Lighthill had described his own report as showing "how AI appears to a lay person after two months spent looking through the literature", representing "only the personal view of the author". And sociologists studying the perceptron controversy argue that Minsky and Papert's book got its reputation as the neural-network killer largely from timing: it landed just as rival approaches were winning the competition for money and people, so almost nobody was left to argue back.

Never was one document powerful enough to kill a field on its own. What it could do was give an already nervous funder a reason.

## Key Vocabulary

- **blunt** — direct and honest, in a way that may seem harsh. *Her assessment was blunt but fair.*
- **to evaporate** — to disappear quickly and completely. *Investor confidence evaporated within a year.*
- **verdict** — an official judgement or decision. *The committee delivered its verdict in July.*
- **lay person** — someone without specialist knowledge of a subject. *The report was written for a lay person, not an expert.*
- **to argue back** — to respond to criticism by defending your position. *By then there was nobody left to argue back.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Should someone from outside a field be trusted to judge it? What do outsiders see that insiders miss — and what do they get wrong?
2. Rosenblatt's machine worked; the claims made about it did not. Who is responsible when technology is oversold — the inventor, the press, or the audience?
3. Have you seen a technology in your own lifetime that was hyped, dismissed, and then quietly became normal?
4. If funding for something is cut for ten years, what is lost besides the research itself?

## Quick Recap

After extravagant claims about early neural networks, two corrections arrived: Minsky and Papert's mathematical critique in 1969, and Sir James Lighthill's damning 1972 report, which blamed the "combinatorial explosion". Funding collapsed, and again in 1987. But Lighthill called his own report a personal view written by a non-expert, and historians doubt either document caused the winter on its own.
