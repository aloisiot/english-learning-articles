## Grammar Spotlight: Indirect Questions

An indirect question is a question folded inside another sentence. Two things change: the word order goes back to normal statement order, and *do/does/did* disappears. Compare **What does he know?** with **She asked what he knew.** When there is no question word, *if* or *whether* takes its place: **Is anyone wiser? → He asked whether anyone was wiser.**

- Direct: *What is courage?* → Indirect: He wanted to know **what courage was**.
- Direct: *Do you understand?* → Indirect: She asked **whether I understood**.
- Direct: *Why did they agree?* → Indirect: Nobody could explain **why they had agreed**.

## A Method Built Entirely Out of Other People's Answers

Socrates wrote nothing. Everything we have about him was written by other people, which is why scholars still argue about which ideas were his and which belong to his student Plato. They call it the Socratic problem, and it never quite goes away.

What survives clearly is a method. The Greeks called it *elenchus* — putting to the test, or refutation — and it worked like this. Socrates would find someone confident, usually a politician, a poet, or a craftsman with opinions about virtue, and ask what justice was, or piety, or courage. The man would answer. Socrates would then ask a series of small follow-up questions: clarifications, examples, edge cases. Each one seemed harmless. Then he would show that the answers taken together contradicted the original definition.

The important detail is that Socrates never supplied a premise of his own. He argued only from what the other man had already agreed to. Nobody could complain about being told what to think, because he never told anyone anything. He compared himself to a midwife: he had no ideas of his own, he said, and only helped other people give birth to theirs.

Most of these conversations end in *aporia* — a dead end, no definition reached. That was the point. Socrates' friend Chaerephon once asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the oracle said no one was. Socrates found this baffling, since he was painfully aware of how little he knew, and he set out to prove the oracle wrong by finding somebody wiser. He questioned everyone with a reputation for wisdom. He concluded that he was wiser than them in exactly one respect: he did not claim to know what he did not know.

It is worth asking what it costs to do this to people in public, day after day, in a small city. In 399 BC the Athenians tried him, and executed him. Plato's *Apology* claims to record what he said in his own defence.

He left behind no doctrine — only an unanswered question. *What is justice, really?* His student Plato spent a career building an answer.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to refute** — to prove that a statement or person is wrong. *He refuted the definition using the man's own words.*
- **premise** — a statement accepted as true, used as the basis of an argument. *Socrates introduced no premise the other man had not already accepted.*
- **dead end** — a point beyond which no further progress is possible. *The conversation reached a dead end, which was often the intention.*
- **to be baffled** — to be completely confused by something. *He was baffled to hear that no one was wiser than he was.*
- **doctrine** — a set of beliefs taught by a person or group. *He left no doctrine behind, only a method.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Socrates never stated his own position — he only asked questions. Is that an honest way to argue, or a way of avoiding responsibility for what you believe?
2. He claimed his only advantage was knowing what he didn't know. Do you know anyone like that? Is it a useful quality at work?
3. Athens executed a man for asking too many questions. What does that tell you about how societies respond to people who question them publicly?
4. Can you think of a belief you hold that you have never actually been asked to define?

## Quick Recap

Socrates wrote nothing, so what is genuinely his is disputed. His method, the elenchus, was to take someone's confident definition of a virtue and, using only that person's own answers, show it contradicted itself. Most conversations ended without a definition — and the unanswered question of what things like justice really are is what Plato inherited.
