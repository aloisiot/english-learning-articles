## Grammar Spotlight: Nominalisation

Nominalisation turns a verb into a noun: *abolish* becomes *the abolition of*, *grant* becomes *the granting of*. It makes writing more formal and more compact — and it quietly removes the person doing the action. That second effect is worth noticing, because it is exactly how some historical claims get made.

- Who did it: Isabel **signed** the law. → Nominalised: **the signing** of the law
- Who did it: People **escaped** in growing numbers. → Nominalised: **the escape** of growing numbers
- Notice: *The abolition of slavery came in 1888* tells you when. It does not tell you who.

## The Word "Granted" Is Doing a Lot of Work

Every Brazilian schoolchild learns the date: 13 May 1888, the *Lei Áurea*, signed by Princess Isabel, freeing around 700,000 people. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. The story is usually told as an act of royal generosity — the granting of freedom from above.

Look at the two years before the signature and a different picture appears. From 1886 onwards, enslaved people were leaving the coffee plantations of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro in growing numbers. One contemporary description captures the shift precisely: they ran away "at first secretly one by one, and later in mass and almost publicly." Networks of fugitives established themselves around towns such as Santos and Campos. Through 1887 the pace of revolts accelerated, some involving large groups from neighbouring estates at once. Quilombos — communities founded by people who had escaped — were not only a colonial phenomenon safely in the past; new ones were still being created on the eve of abolition, and they frightened planters and provincial authorities alike.

Alongside this, an organised movement worked in three arenas at once. Luiz Gama, a lawyer who had been born free and illegally sold into slavery as a child, won large numbers of freedom suits by pushing the legal definition of lawful enslavement to its limit. José do Patrocínio, a pharmacist turned journalist, was the movement's public voice. André Rebouças, an engineer and professor, was the insider who could reach ministers; with Patrocínio he founded the Abolitionist Confederation.

By May 1888 the institution was already failing where it mattered economically. The law confirmed a collapse rather than causing one.

There is a final twist. Historians note that the story of abolition as the Crown's gift was constructed *after* the event — and that Rebouças and Patrocínio themselves helped build it. The myth that erases the movement was partly the work of the movement.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to grant** — to give something formally, especially by authority. *Freedom was described as something granted.*
- **fugitive** — someone who has escaped and is being pursued. *Fugitive networks formed around the coffee towns.*
- **estate** — a large area of land under one owner. *Revolts spread between neighbouring estates.*
- **to accelerate** — to become faster. *The pace of escapes accelerated through 1887.*
- **twist** — an unexpected turn in a story. *There is a final twist to the story.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Why do you think the "Princess Isabel freed the slaves" version became the official story, and who did it serve?
2. Two abolitionists helped build a myth that left themselves out. What might explain that?
3. The law gave freedom but no land, money or guarantees. What difference does that make to what happened next?
4. How was 13 May taught in your school, and has the way Brazilians mark that date changed in your lifetime?

## Quick Recap

The Lei Áurea of 1888 freed about 700,000 people and is usually credited to Princess Isabel. But mass escapes from the coffee plantations, accelerating revolts through 1887, quilombos still being founded, and an organised movement led in part by Luiz Gama, José do Patrocínio and André Rebouças had already made slavery unworkable. The law confirmed a collapse it did not cause.
