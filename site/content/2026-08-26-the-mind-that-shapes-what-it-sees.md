## Grammar Spotlight: Correlative Conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions come in pairs and join two elements of equal weight: *both … and*, *either … or*, *neither … nor*, *not only … but also*. The rule that catches people out is parallel structure — whatever follows the first half must match the grammar of what follows the second.

- Balanced: The mind is **both** a receiver **and** an organiser.
- Wrong: *He was neither convinced by reason nor by experience.* → Right: He was convinced **neither** by reason **nor** by experience.
- Emphatic: **Not only** did the senses supply the content, **but** the mind **also** supplied the form.

## Rescuing Knowledge by Limiting It

Descartes had tried to build certainty out of pure reasoning. A century later David Hume attacked that whole approach from the other direction, arguing that we cannot get to necessary truths about the world by reason alone — we only ever observe one thing following another, and habit does the rest.

Kant read him and, by his own account, it broke something open. Writing later in the *Prolegomena*, he freely confessed that it was David Hume who first, many years ago, interrupted his dogmatic slumber — and he added that Hume's attack on metaphysics had been more decisive for its fate than any other event since metaphysics began. Exactly which of Hume's arguments did it is still disputed: some scholars point to the attack on causation, others to the attack on the principle of sufficient reason underneath rationalist metaphysics as a whole. Kant is not perfectly clear, and honest accounts leave it open.

The trap he saw was that neither side worked alone. Rationalism pushed to its limit becomes an elaborate exercise in logic with nothing to say about the actual world; empiricism pushed to its limit collapses into a scepticism that cannot even guarantee that nature will behave tomorrow as it did today. Both positions were unlivable, and each was right about what was wrong with the other.

His solution inverts the usual assumption. Instead of supposing that our knowledge must conform to objects, suppose that objects must conform to our knowledge. He compared it to what Copernicus did — stop assuming the observer sits still at the centre. Experience supplies all the content of knowledge, and the mind is not a passive surface receiving it. Space, time and the categories are the structure the mind itself brings, the conditions that make organised experience possible at all. That is how there can be judgments that are both informative and necessarily true — the synthetic a priori judgments the *Critique of Pure Reason* of 1781 exists to explain.

Every price has to be paid somewhere, and here is the bill. If the order we find in the world is partly the order our minds impose, then we know the world as it appears under those conditions — never as it is independently of them. The thing in itself is real and permanently beyond us.

Kant regarded that limit as the honest conclusion. Not everyone agreed that a thing you can say nothing whatever about deserves to be called a thing at all.

## Key Vocabulary

- **slumber** — sleep, often used figuratively for a lack of awareness. *He described his earlier work as a dogmatic slumber.*
- **to invert** — to reverse the position or order of something. *Kant inverted the assumption that knowledge conforms to objects.*
- **unlivable** — impossible to sustain in practice. *Both extremes were unlivable, though each had a point.*
- **to impose** — to force something onto something else. *Part of the order we find is order the mind imposes.*
- **to conform to** — to match or fit with something. *He proposed that objects conform to our knowledge, not the reverse.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Kant argued that part of the order we see in the world is put there by us. Does that feel true to you, or like a trick?
2. He rescued knowledge by accepting a permanent limit on it. Is that a fair trade?
3. Scholars still disagree about what exactly Hume said that changed Kant's mind. Does it matter, if we can see the result?
4. Are there things you think are real but genuinely unknowable? How would you argue for them?

## Quick Recap

Hume's attack on rationalism woke Kant from what he called his dogmatic slumber, though which argument did it remains disputed. Kant's answer was that experience supplies the content of knowledge while the mind supplies its structure — so objects conform to our knowledge rather than the other way round. The price is that things as they are in themselves, apart from those conditions, are permanently beyond knowing.
