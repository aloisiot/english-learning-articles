## Grammar Spotlight: Defining Relative Clauses

A defining relative clause tells us **which one** we mean. It uses *who*, *which* or *that*, takes no commas, and cannot be removed without leaving the sentence unclear. (Compare the non-defining kind, which sits between commas and merely adds an aside.)

- The prince **who declared independence** was heir to the Portuguese throne. — tells us which prince
- The fleet **that left Lisbon in 1807** carried the entire government. — tells us which fleet
- Compare: Pedro, **who was twenty-three at the time**, declared independence. — an aside about a man we have already identified

## The Empire That Sailed Across an Ocean

In November 1807 a French army was advancing on Lisbon. The decision that followed has no real parallel in European history: rather than surrender or fight, the Portuguese court packed itself onto ships and left. On 29 November the fleet sailed, escorted by the British navy, carrying the Prince Regent Dom João — who was ruling on behalf of his mother, Queen Maria I — along with somewhere between ten and fifteen thousand courtiers, officials and servants.

They arrived in Rio de Janeiro on 7 March 1808. The city that received them had perhaps fifty thousand inhabitants and was entirely unprepared. Overnight, a colonial port became the seat of a European empire.

The consequences were permanent. Brazil gained the institutions that a capital requires — courts, a treasury, a printing press, a military academy — and won the right to trade directly with other nations instead of only through Lisbon. Dom João stayed thirteen years, returning to Portugal only in April 1821, leaving his son Pedro behind.

What happened next is the part that surprises people who know the Spanish American story. Portugal wanted the old arrangement back. The Brazilian elites who had grown used to running their own affairs did not. Advisers around Pedro — José Bonifácio de Andrada e Silva among them — urged him to break with Portugal himself, before separatist movements did it without him. On 7 September 1822 he declared independence. In October he was proclaimed Emperor Pedro I; in December he was crowned. Spanish America had fractured into republics. Brazil stayed one country, and its head of state was the heir to the throne it had just left.

It is often called a bloodless independence. That is not quite true. Portuguese garrisons held out in Bahia, Maranhão and Pará, and Pedro hired foreign officers, including the British admiral Thomas Cochrane, to dislodge them. Portugal recognised the new empire only in August 1825, nearly three years later.

## Key Vocabulary

- **court** — a monarch's household and government. *The entire court sailed to Brazil.*
- **regent** — someone who rules on behalf of a monarch. *Dom João governed as prince regent.*
- **garrison** — troops stationed in a particular place. *Portuguese garrisons resisted in the north.*
- **to dislodge** — to force someone out of a position. *Foreign officers were hired to dislodge them.*
- **parallel** — a comparable case. *The move has no real parallel in European history.*

## Discussion Questions

1. A capital city moving to its own colony is almost unheard of. What do you think it did to how Brazilians saw themselves?
2. Independence was declared by a prince rather than won by a movement. Does that make it feel like a different kind of event?
3. Spanish America split into many countries; Brazil stayed one. How much do you think the monarchy explains that?
4. Why do you think the phrase "independence without bloodshed" has lasted, when there was fighting in several provinces?

## Quick Recap

Fleeing Napoleon, the Portuguese court sailed to Rio in 1807-08 and stayed thirteen years, turning a colony into the seat of an empire. When Portugal tried to reverse this, Prince Pedro declared independence in September 1822 and became emperor — keeping Brazil united under a monarch while Spanish America fragmented into republics. Fighting continued until Portugal recognised the new state in 1825.
