## Grammar Spotlight: Non-defining Relative Clauses

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information about something already identified. It sits between commas, and — unlike a defining clause — it can be removed without changing who or what we are talking about. Use *which*, *who* or *whose*; never *that*, and never omit the pronoun.

- Defining: The database **that DeepMind built** is free. (tells us *which* database)
- Non-defining: AlphaFold, **which DeepMind built**, is free. (we already know which; this is an aside)
- Non-defining: John Jumper, **who shared the 2024 Nobel Prize**, credits decades of public data.

## The Quiet Case for the Defence

Proteins do almost everything in a living body, and what a protein does depends on the shape it folds into. Working out that shape used to mean months of laboratory work. Over roughly sixty years, using techniques such as X-ray crystallography, biologists determined the structures of about 190,000 proteins.

Then came AlphaFold, which was built by the London company DeepMind and predicts a protein's structure from its sequence. Its database, which launched in July 2021 with about 360,000 predictions, now holds around 200 million structures covering more than a million organisms. The predictions were released freely through a European public institute, and have been used by over a million researchers in nearly every country. In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded half to David Baker for computational protein design, and half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction.

Why it matters is concrete rather than abstract. Knowing a protein's shape suggests what it does and which drugs might act on it; working out the structures of the SARS-CoV-2 proteins, for instance, helped researchers understand the virus and develop vaccines.

The temptation is to read this as machines replacing scientists. Jumper himself tells it differently: "Public data were essential to the development of AlphaFold," he said, describing the carefully curated results of "an entire subfield of biology" as exactly what let the system generalise. The machine learned from sixty years of patient human measurement, which somebody had chosen to share.

The same reversal shows up elsewhere. Machine translation, which Lighthill wrote off in 1972 as money spent for almost nothing, is now so ordinary we barely notice it. And speech recognition and captioning are counted by the World Health Organization among assistive products — needed today by more than 2.5 billion people, a number expected to reach 3.5 billion by 2050. Most of those who need them still cannot get them.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to fold** — to bend into a particular shape. *A protein folds into a structure that determines its function.*
- **to curate** — to select and organise material carefully. *Decades of results had been curated by hand.*
- **to generalise** — to apply what was learned from examples to new cases. *The model generalises well to unfamiliar proteins.*
- **temptation** — an urge to do something you probably shouldn't. *There's a temptation to overstate what the technology did.*
- **assistive** — designed to help someone with a disability. *Captioning is classed as an assistive technology.*

## Discussion Questions

1. AlphaFold's results were given away free. Should discoveries built on decades of publicly funded data always be released that way?
2. Jumper credits the scientists whose measurements trained the system. When a machine learns from other people's work, who should get the credit?
3. Billions of people need assistive technology and most cannot get it. Is the barrier really technological?
4. Which strikes you as more valuable — a tool that helps a few thousand specialists enormously, or one that helps millions slightly?

## Quick Recap

Structural biology took about sixty years to determine 190,000 protein structures; AlphaFold's free database now holds roughly 200 million, work recognised by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Its creators stress it was only possible because that earlier human work had been carefully curated and shared — and machine translation, once the field's worst failure, is now unremarkable.
