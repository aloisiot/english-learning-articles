## Grammar Spotlight: Reporting Passives

When a claim comes from research rather than from us, English often puts it in a passive construction with a reporting verb: *it is thought that*, *is believed to have been*, *are said to be*. This does two jobs at once — it credits the finding to the field rather than to a named person, and it signals that the matter is not fully settled.

- Direct: Researchers think the soils were made deliberately.
- Reporting passive: The soils **are now thought** to have been made deliberately.
- Direct: Scholars once believed the forest was empty.
- Reporting passive: The forest **was long believed** to have been empty.

## What the Lasers Found Under the Trees

For most of the last five hundred years, the Amazon was described in European accounts as wilderness — a forest so vast and so hostile that no large society could ever have lived there. That description shaped everything from colonial policy to modern conservation. It is now thought to be wrong.

Two lines of evidence changed the picture. The first is soil. Across the basin there are patches of unusually dark, fertile earth, full of broken pottery, known in Brazil as *terra preta*. These are believed to have been created by people — the residue of centuries of settlement, cooking fires and deliberate soil management. Estimates of how much ground they cover vary enormously, from six thousand square kilometres to well over a hundred thousand, and the disagreement is worth noting: a minority of researchers argue the fertility could be natural, left by river sediment, with people simply settling where the good soil already was. Most specialists reject this, but the question is not formally closed.

The second line of evidence is shape. In the state of Acre, more than 450 geometric ditched enclosures have been found across roughly 13,000 square kilometres, built between about 2,000 and 650 years ago. Crucially, they were not cut out of virgin forest. They are reported to have been built inside woodland that had already been managed for millennia, using small clearings rather than mass felling.

Then, in July 2026, a study in *Nature* flew 4,430 kilometres of laser survey capable of seeing through the canopy. One cultural area alone, it found, contains as many earthworks as had previously been estimated for the entire Amazon. Its authors propose that between 1.25 and 3 million people lived there around 100 to 300 AD, and suggest that if the pattern holds elsewhere, our models of the forest — its soils, its biodiversity, even its role in the global climate — may need rethinking.

The forest Europeans called untouched had been someone's home for a very long time.

## Key Vocabulary

- **wilderness** — land with no human settlement or cultivation. *The region was described as wilderness for centuries.*
- **residue** — what is left behind after a process. *The dark soil is the residue of long settlement.*
- **sediment** — material carried and deposited by water. *River sediment can enrich soil naturally.*
- **enclosure** — an area surrounded by a ditch, wall or fence. *Over 450 enclosures have been mapped in Acre.*
- **canopy** — the layer formed by the tops of tall trees. *The laser could see through the canopy to the ground.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Why do you think the idea of an empty Amazon lasted so long? Who benefited from it?
2. If a place was managed by people for thousands of years, does that change what "protecting" it should mean today?
3. Archaeologists disagree about how the dark soils formed. How comfortable are you with history that isn't settled?
4. What else do you think we assume about the past simply because nobody has looked properly yet?

## Quick Recap

The Amazon was long described as untouched wilderness, but dark fertile soils and hundreds of geometric earthworks suggest large managed populations before 1500. A 2026 laser survey found one region alone holds as many earthworks as previously estimated for the whole basin, and proposes a population of 1.25 to 3 million there in the early centuries AD.
