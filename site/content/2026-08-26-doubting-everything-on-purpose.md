## Grammar Spotlight: Subjunctive and Unreal Past

To talk about situations that are not real — suppositions, wishes, hypotheses — English shifts the tense backwards. After *suppose*, *imagine*, *what if*, *as if*, *I wish* and *if only*, a past form describes a present unreality. Formal English also keeps *were* for all persons: **if I were deceived**, not *was*.

- Supposition: **Suppose that everything you saw were an illusion.**
- Comparison: He argued **as if the world were a dream**.
- Wish: **I wish I knew** which of my beliefs were false.

## Starting Again From Nothing

There is a long silence in this story. Nearly two thousand years separate Aristotle from the next argument in it, and nothing in what follows is a reply to Aristotle's own writing. What Descartes reacted against was what Aristotle's thought had become by the 1600s: the Scholastic philosophy taught in the schools, including the Jesuit college where he was educated.

His complaint was specific. That tradition allowed for what it called probable cognition — conclusions good enough to work with, especially about the natural world, and grounded ultimately in the senses. Descartes thought anything grounded in the senses was open to doubt, and he wanted no part of it. We reject all merely probable cognition, he wrote, and resolve to believe only what is perfectly known and incapable of being doubted.

So he set out to doubt on purpose. The method was to treat as false any belief vulnerable to even the slightest doubt — not because he thought everything was false, but to clear the ground. In the *Meditations on First Philosophy* of 1641 he pushes this as far as it will go. The senses deceive us sometimes, so they might deceive us always. He might be dreaming right now; nothing in the experience would tell him. And suppose there were a deceiver of enormous power, devoting all its energy to misleading him — then the external world, his own body, even arithmetic could be an elaborate fraud.

What survives is small and strange. If the demon is deceiving him, something is being deceived. He would have to exist in order to be fooled at all. The attempt to doubt his own existence defeats itself, because the doubting is itself a thought, and a thought needs a thinker. The phrase everyone quotes — *cogito, ergo sum* — actually comes from the earlier *Discourse on Method* of 1637; the argument itself is made in the second Meditation.

The certainty rests on thinking in the widest sense: doubting, affirming, denying, willing, imagining. Any of them will do. What Descartes has left, after demolishing everything, is a thinking thing that cannot doubt itself away — and the enormous problem of getting from there back out to the world.

He is usually called the father of modern philosophy for this. The rebuilding project he started is what the next argument in the series inherits.

## Key Vocabulary

- **to demolish** — to destroy something completely, usually a structure. *He demolished his own beliefs deliberately, one at a time.*
- **vulnerable to** — capable of being harmed or attacked by something. *Any belief vulnerable to doubt was set aside as false.*
- **elaborate** — complicated and carefully constructed. *The deception would have to be extraordinarily elaborate.*
- **to clear the ground** — to remove obstacles so something new can be built. *The doubt was meant to clear the ground, not to end in despair.*
- **to defeat itself** — to fail because of its own nature. *Doubting your own existence defeats itself.*

## Discussion Questions

1. Descartes deliberately assumed everything he believed was false. Would that be a useful exercise for anyone outside philosophy — or just paralysing?
2. He rejected "good enough to work with" in favour of absolute certainty. Which standard do you actually use in your own decisions?
3. The evil demon is four hundred years old, and simulation arguments today look very similar. Why do you think the idea keeps coming back?
4. Descartes ended up certain of one thing: that he was thinking. Is that enough to build anything on?

## Quick Recap

Descartes broke not with Aristotle directly but with the Scholastic tradition built on him, rejecting its sense-based "probable cognition" in favour of absolute certainty. His method of doubt discards anything open to the slightest doubt, pushed to the extreme by the dream argument and the evil demon. The one belief that survives is that he is thinking, and therefore exists — which leaves him the problem of getting back out to the world.
