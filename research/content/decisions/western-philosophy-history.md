# Western Philosophy — A History of Ideas — decisions

Why this series is written the way it is. **Kept permanently**, including
after the series ships — this is what a future revision needs so it does
not undo a deliberate choice.

Progress tracking for this series lives (while it is unfinished) in
[`../progress/western-philosophy-history.md`](../progress/western-philosophy-history.md).

## Theme & throughline

Six thinkers, in chronological order, where each one's central move is a
documented reaction to the one before — not a curated set of "great
philosophers" grouped by era. The connecting question is what knowledge
rests on, and each article ends with the problem the next one inherits.

## Care needed — constraints that must survive a revision

- **Only real connections.** Explicit user instruction (2026-08-26):
  assert a link between two thinkers only where sources support it. Every
  connective claim in these articles traces to a source listed below. Do
  not add smoother connective tissue in a revision.
- **The Aristotle → Descartes gap is deliberate.** Roughly 2000 years,
  no direct textual link. The connection kept is narrow and specific:
  Descartes reacts against the *Scholastic* Aristotelian teaching of his
  own education, not against Aristotle's texts directly. Do not rewrite
  this into an unbroken lineage — article 4 names the gap out loud.
- **Independent from "Eastern Philosophy and the Self."** Different
  question (knowledge, not selfhood), no shared subjects, no mirrored
  per-article shape. An earlier draft of this series proposed mirroring
  it (Descartes/Hume/Sartre on the self) and the user rejected that —
  do not reintroduce it.
- **Hume appears only as the trigger for Kant, and is not a subject.**
  He earns a mention in article 5 because Kant names him; giving him his
  own article would break the six-link chain, since nothing in the
  series reacts to Hume directly except Kant.
- **Nietzsche is the end of the arc, not a seventh "and then modern
  philosophy" summary.** The series stops where the metaphysical
  scaffolding is rejected. Do not append a 20th-century wrap-up article.

## Research notes

### Subject 1 — Socrates

**Sources:**

- [Socrates — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/socrates/)
- [Socrates — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/socrates/)
- [Plato's Shorter Ethical Works — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-ethics-shorter/)

**Facts the article can use:**

- The *elenchus* (Greek: "putting to the test", "refutation") is
  Socrates' method. Structure: the respondent states a thesis he claims
  to know; Socrates asks questions eliciting clarifications and
  extensions; Socrates then shows the original thesis is inconsistent
  with what the respondent himself has just agreed to.
- Crucially, Socrates argues only from the interlocutor's own beliefs —
  he does not import premises of his own. The refutation is the
  respondent contradicting himself.
- Socrates compares himself to a midwife: he has no ideas of his own but
  helps others give birth to theirs.
- Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than
  Socrates; the oracle said no one was. Socrates, aware of his own
  ignorance, set out to refute this by finding someone wiser, questioning
  people with reputations for wisdom — and concluded he was wiser only in
  that he did not claim to know what he did not know.
- Trial and death: Athens, 399 BC. Plato's *Apology* presents itself as
  his defence speech.
- The "Socratic problem": Socrates wrote nothing, so what is Socratic and
  what is Plato's own is a live scholarly dispute. **Say this in the
  article** — it matters for article 2, and overclaiming here would be a
  real error.
- Most questions the elenchus pursues are "What is X?" about virtues —
  justice, piety, courage — and typically end in *aporia*, no answer
  reached.

**Connection forward:** the unanswered "What is X?" question is exactly
what Plato's Forms are built to answer.

### Subject 2 — Plato

**Sources:**

- [Plato's Middle Period Metaphysics and Epistemology — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-metaphysics/)
- [Plato's Shorter Ethical Works — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-ethics-shorter/)
- [Substance — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/substance/)

**Facts the article can use:**

- Plato's metaphysics starts from Socrates' investigation into
  universals — Justice, Piety, Courage — pursued through the elenchus,
  with the "What is X?" question at its heart.
- The Theory of Forms was developed to answer the problem the elenchus
  kept running into: the validity of definitions. Forms are stable
  essences that transcend particular examples and opinions.
- Forms are eternal and unchanging archetypes; sensible objects
  "participate in" or imitate them and are imperfect, changing copies.
- Per SEP: for Plato the entities that best merit the title "beings" are
  the intelligible Forms, which material objects imperfectly copy.
- The theory does not appear fully formed. It emerges gradually as Plato
  develops Socratic questioning into a systematic metaphysics, becoming
  central in the middle dialogues — *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*.
- In those middle dialogues the character "Socrates" is less the
  historical man and more a mouthpiece for Plato's own developing
  doctrine: more didactic, advancing positive theories rather than only
  questioning and refuting. (This is the payoff of the "Socratic problem"
  flagged in article 1.)
- The Divided Line (*Republic* Book 6) splits reality into the visible
  world of images and shadows and the intelligible world of Forms. The
  Cave (Book 7) maps onto it: prisoners take shadows for reality; the
  sun outside stands for the Form of the Good.
- Plato founded the Academy in Athens around 387 BC and taught there
  until his death in 348 BC. Aristotle studied there for about twenty
  years — the link to article 3 is literal, not just intellectual.

**Connection forward:** Aristotle attacks precisely the *separateness*
of the Forms.

### Subject 3 — Aristotle

**Sources:**

- [Aristotle's Metaphysics — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-metaphysics/)
- [Form vs. Matter — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/form-matter/)
- [Substance — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/substance/)

**Facts the article can use:**

- Aristotle's objection, per SEP: Plato's Forms are "a bastard confusion
  of universal and particular" — they are supposed to be universal, yet
  treated as individual things existing on their own.
- He is "eager to distance himself" from Forms that exist apart from the
  material world, and does so by insisting his own forms are *enmeshed
  in matter* — hylomorphism: an ordinary object is form and matter
  together, not a copy of something stored elsewhere.
- The Third Man Argument: if a Form is needed to explain what particular
  men have in common, another Form is needed to explain what the Form
  and the particulars have in common — and so on without end.
- Aristotle's account of substance has two parts: the basic account in
  the *Categories*, and the analysis of material substances into matter
  and form in the *Physics* and *Metaphysics* Z.
- Consequence: knowledge starts from observing things themselves rather
  than reasoning about a separate realm. Universals are abstracted from
  particulars, not glimpsed behind them.
- **Care:** do not overstate this as "Aristotle invented science" or
  frame him as a modern empiricist. He rejects Plato's *separation* of
  Forms; he does not reject forms, essences, or teleology.

**Connection forward:** the medieval Scholastic tradition built on
Aristotle is the thing Descartes breaks with — the link is via that
tradition, not directly.

### Subject 4 — Descartes

**Sources:**

- [Descartes' Method — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-method/)
- [Descartes' Epistemology — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-epistemology/)
- [Descartes, René — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/rene-descartes/)
- [Cogito, ergo sum — Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cogito-ergo-sum)

**Facts the article can use:**

- Descartes' break with Scholastic-Aristotelian philosophy is twofold:
  he thought its method was vulnerable to doubt because it relied on
  sensation, and he wanted to replace its final-causal model with a
  mechanistic one.
- The sharpest contrast, per SEP: Aristotelians make room for "probable
  cognition," especially in natural philosophy. Descartes: "we reject all
  merely probable cognition and resolve to believe only what is perfectly
  known and incapable of being doubted."
- Method of doubt: treat as false any belief open to even the slightest
  doubt — not destructive scepticism but a clearing operation.
- *Meditations on First Philosophy*, 1641. Meditation I raises the dream
  argument and the evil-demon hypothesis: a supremely powerful deceiver
  who makes the external world, the senses, and even mathematics
  illusory.
- The cogito survives it. Per Britannica: even if an all-powerful demon
  tried to deceive him into thinking he exists when he does not, he would
  have to exist in order to be deceived. The phrase "cogito, ergo sum"
  was coined in the *Discourse on Method* (1637); the argument is made in
  Meditation II (1641). **Keep these two works straight** — a common
  error is attributing the phrase to the *Meditations*.
- Certainty depends on *cogitatio* — thinking/consciousness broadly. Any
  mode of thinking will do: doubting, affirming, denying, willing,
  understanding, imagining.
- He is commonly called the father of modern philosophy for this break.
- **The 2000-year gap:** name it in the article. Nothing here reacts to
  Aristotle's own texts; it reacts to what Aristotle's tradition had
  become by the 1600s.

**Connection forward:** the rationalist project Descartes launches is
what Hume's empiricism attacks, and Kant's reaction to *that* is
article 5.

### Subject 5 — Kant

**Sources:**

- [Immanuel Kant — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)
- [Descartes' Epistemology — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-epistemology/)
- [Abraham Anderson, *Kant, Hume, and the Interruption of Dogmatic Slumber* — Oxford University Press](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/kant-hume-and-the-interruption-of-dogmatic-slumber-9780190096748)

**Facts the article can use:**

- Kant's own words: "It was my recollection of David Hume that broke into
  my dogmatic slumber." He also wrote that Hume's attack on metaphysics
  "was more decisive for its fate than any other event" since metaphysics
  began.
- What exactly woke him is **disputed** among scholars: on one reading it
  was Hume's argument that causal relations cannot be known by pure
  reason; on another (Anderson) it was Hume's attack on the principle of
  sufficient reason, the basis of rationalist metaphysics and the
  cosmological argument. **Report this as disputed** — do not pick a side
  as settled fact.
- The problem as Kant framed it: rationalism pushed to its limit becomes
  empty logic disconnected from reality; empiricism pushed to its limit
  collapses into scepticism that cannot guarantee even natural laws.
- His answer: experience is the origin and limit of knowledge, but the
  conditions that make experience possible lie in the subject, a priori.
  The mind is not a passive receiver — it supplies the forms and
  categories that organise experience.
- The "Copernican revolution": instead of assuming knowledge must conform
  to objects, suppose objects must conform to our knowledge.
- The central question: how are synthetic a priori judgments possible —
  judgments that are both informative and necessarily true?
- *Critique of Pure Reason*, 1781.
- The cost: what things are like independently of the conditions of our
  experience — the thing in itself — is placed beyond knowledge.

**Connection forward:** that leftover, the thing in itself, is exactly
what Nietzsche attacks.

### Subject 6 — Nietzsche

**Sources:**

- [Friedrich Nietzsche — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/)
- [Nietzsche, Friedrich — Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/nietzsch/)
- [Nietzsche's critique of Kant's thing in itself — PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/RICNCO)
- [Nietzsche's Criticism of Kant's Categorical Imperative: An Inquiry](https://pfk.qom.ac.ir/article_2369.html?lang=en)

**Facts the article can use:**

- Nietzsche rejects the thing in itself as self-contradictory: strip away
  every property knowable through experience and what remains is the
  incoherent idea of a propertyless thing.
- On the categorical imperative: the irony he presses is that Kant's
  "critique" took the form of *legislation*. Kant's critique never
  questioned the value of knowledge and moral ideals themselves.
- Kant grounds morality in reason and sets desire and instinct aside;
  Nietzsche puts instinct and will to power in place of duty.
- Perspectivism: there is no absolute, "God's eye" standpoint from which
  everything can be surveyed. This is the direct denial of what the whole
  chain — Forms, certainty, the a priori — had been reaching for.
- "God is dead" appears in *The Gay Science* (1882). Per SEP, it is not
  celebration but a challenge to his readers' complacency: a condemnation
  of absolute perspectives and values, redirecting attention to the
  existing world and away from "escapist, pain-relieving, heavenly
  otherworlds."
- **Care:** do not present "God is dead" as simple atheist triumphalism,
  and do not present will to power as a political doctrine. Both are
  well-documented misreadings.

**Connection back:** closes the series by rejecting the shared assumption
running from Plato onward — that behind appearances there is a stable,
knowable order.

## Subjects, order, and grammar assignment

| # | Subject | Grammar focus | Why this point fits |
|---|---------|----------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Socrates | Indirect questions | The article is about questioning; indirect questions are what reporting an interrogation actually requires. |
| 2 | Plato | Nominalization | Forms are abstractions turned into nouns — the grammar mirrors the move. |
| 3 | Aristotle | Reduced relative clauses | Dense descriptive prose about things and their properties; distinct from non-defining relative clauses (already used site-wide). |
| 4 | Descartes | Subjunctive and unreal past | Methodic doubt is sustained hypothesis: *if I were being deceived…* |
| 5 | Kant | Correlative conjunctions | *Both … and*, *neither … nor* — the grammar of a synthesis of two positions. |
| 6 | Nietzsche | Result and purpose clauses | *so … that*, *such … that* — stating consequences, which is what the article is about. |

**Grammar rotation check (2026-08-26).** Already used site-wide and
therefore avoided: third and mixed conditionals, reported speech,
participle clauses, passive voice, cleft sentences, concessive clauses,
hedging and modal probability, modal perfect, inversion for emphasis,
non-defining relative clauses, phrasal verbs. All six points above are
