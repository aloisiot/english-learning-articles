# Brazil, From Above — decisions

Why this series is written the way it is. **Kept permanently**, including
after the series ships — this is what a future revision needs so it does
not undo a deliberate choice.

Progress tracking for this series lives (while it is unfinished) in
[`../progress/brazil-from-above.md`](../progress/brazil-from-above.md).

## Theme & throughline

Brazil changed its political form five times in two centuries — colony,
empire, republic, dictatorship, democracy — and almost every one of those
transitions was negotiated from above rather than won from below. The
series follows that pattern chronologically, and deliberately lets one
article argue against it.

## Register — decided, not default

**The articles are written for a reader who does not know Brazil**, and
explain events from scratch.

This was an explicit choice by the user, against the alternative of
assuming knowledge and focusing only on vocabulary and framing. The
reasoning matters for anyone tempted to "correct" it later: the learner
is Brazilian and already knows this history, so the articles are not
teaching him the events — they are giving him the full English scaffolding
for facts he already holds, in the form he would need to explain them to
someone else. Writing them as insider notes would strip out exactly the
connective language he needs.

Practical consequence: do not skip context because "he knows this". Name
dates, institutions and people as an outsider would need them named.

## Subjects, angles and grammar

### 1. Before 1500 — the forest that was never wild

**Sources (verified live):**

- [Ferreira et al., "Over 20,000 precolonial earthworks in the Southwest Amazonia", *Nature*, 29 July 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10835-7) — primary
- [Watling et al., "Impact of pre-Columbian 'geoglyph' builders on Amazonian forests", *PNAS* 114(8), 2017](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1614359114) — primary
- [Evidence confirms an anthropic origin of Amazonian Dark Earths, *Nature Communications*, 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31064-2)
- [Intentional creation of carbon-rich dark earth soils in the Amazon, *Science Advances*, 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adh8499)
- [A natural origin for Amazonian dark earths? — *Revista Pesquisa FAPESP*](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/a-natural-origin-for-amazonian-dark-earths/) — the dissenting view
- [Geoglyphs of Acre — UNESCO tentative list](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5999/)

**Facts the article can use:**

- *Terra preta* / Amazonian Dark Earths: unusually fertile black soils
  full of ceramic fragments, found at hundreds of sites. Estimated
  coverage varies enormously — commonly cited at 6,000–18,000 km², with
  one modelled estimate above 150,000 km² (~3.2% of the forest). Use the
  range, not a single number.
- The 2017 PNAS study of Acre's geoglyphs: over 450 geometric ditched
  enclosures across roughly 13,000 km², built approximately 2,000–650
  years before present, and — the key finding — constructed *within
  forests already managed for millennia*, using small-scale burning
  rather than clearance. Not deforestation; agroforestry.
- The 2026 *Nature* paper flew **4,430 km of canopy-penetrating LiDAR**
  and found that this one cultural area "contains as many earthworks as
  previously estimated for the whole of Amazonia." It proposes a
  population of **1.25–3 million people in 100–300 AD** for that area
  alone, and argues that if the pattern repeats elsewhere, precolonial
  population density and its effect on soils, forest structure,
  biodiversity "and even some modelling of the global climate should be
  re-evaluated."
- Basin-wide precolumbian population estimates commonly fall in the
  6–10 million range, but this is contested.
- **The dissent is real and should be included**: a 2021 paper argued
  ADE fertility could come from river-deposited nutrients, with people
  settling land that was already fertile rather than creating it. Most
  specialists reject this, but the article should not pretend the
  question is closed.

**Angle.** Opens by unsettling the standard version. Recent archaeology
(dark earths / *terra preta*, geoglyphs and earthworks in Acre,
pre-Columbian settlement density) argues the Amazon was a managed,
populated landscape rather than untouched wilderness. Establishes early
that the familiar story of Brazil is a story someone chose.

**Grammar: reporting passives.** *It is now thought that…*, *the sites
are believed to have been…* — the natural grammar of claims that rest on
contested evidence. Strong form-content fit.

**Care needed.** The revisionist case is strong but not settled;
population estimates in particular vary enormously. Hedge accordingly and
do not swap one confident story for another.

### 2. The colony that received the most enslaved people on earth

**Sources:**

- [SlaveVoyages — Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Estimates](https://www.slavevoyages.org/assessment/estimates) — the authority for all figures below
- [SlaveVoyages — methodology and data completeness (legacy site)](https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-trans-atlantic) — verified live
- [The Transatlantic Slave Trade Database — National Endowment for the Humanities](https://www.neh.gov/project/transatlantic-slave-trade-database)
- [The Atlantic Slave Trade — EHNE encyclopedia, Sorbonne](https://ehne.fr/en/encyclopedia/themes/europe-europeans-and-world/europe-and-atlantic-slave-trade/atlantic-slave-trade)

**Facts the article can use** (see sourcing caveat below):

- Roughly **12.5 million** people were embarked in Africa between 1501
  and 1867; about **10.7 million** survived the crossing and disembarked.
  The gap — more than 1.8 million deaths — is the Middle Passage.
- **Brazil received about 4.86 million**, roughly **45%** of all
  arrivals in the Americas: the single largest destination, and more
  than ten times the number taken to what became the United States
  (~389,000, about 4%).
- The database rests on ~35,000 documented voyages, estimated at 66–80%
  of all transatlantic slaving expeditions. Coverage of the Brazilian
  and Iberian trades after 1750 is described as relatively complete.
- Three export economies in succession — sugar in the sixteenth century,
  gold in the eighteenth, coffee in the nineteenth — each rebuilt on the
  same labour system. The industries changed; the system did not.
- About **700,000 people were still enslaved** when the Lei Áurea was
  signed in 1888 (carry forward to subject 4).

**Sourcing caveat.** slavevoyages.org is a JavaScript-rendered site and
could not be read directly from this environment — the figures above come
from sources citing it (NEH, EHNE, and the database's own legacy
methodology pages, which *were* readable). They are the standard,
widely-corroborated numbers, but **confirm them on the live Estimates
page before drafting**, since that interface allows filtering by
disembarkation region and will give the exact Brazil figure.

**Angle.** Brazil received by far the largest share of the transatlantic
trade, and abolished slavery last in the Americas. Both facts are poorly
known outside Brazil, and together they set up the whole series.

**Grammar: past perfect and narrative tenses.** Three centuries of
sequence needs *had been* / *had already* to keep the order clear.

**Care needed.** Verify figures against the Slave Voyages database rather
than secondary summaries — numbers circulate loosely. Do not let
"the colony received" phrasing turn enslaved people into cargo; name
agency where the sources allow.

### 3. The prince who declared independence from his own father

**Sources:**

- [Independence of Brazil — EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/independence-brazil)
- [Brazilian War of Independence — overview and campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_War_of_Independence)
- [A journey to the new world: the Portuguese court in Brazil — Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/a-journey-to-the-new-world-the-portuguese-court-in-brazil/5AXRumB0octrLg)
- Gomes, Laurentino, *1808* — the standard popular account of the court's flight; worth citing for a Brazilian reader

**Facts the article can use:**

- The fleet left Lisbon on **29 November 1807**, escorted by the British
  navy under the Anglo-Portuguese treaty, carrying the Prince Regent Dom
  João (acting for the incapacitated Queen Maria I) and something like
  **10,000–15,000** courtiers and officials.
- They reached Rio on **7 March 1808**, into a city of roughly 50,000
  people. Dom João stayed **thirteen years**, returning to Lisbon in
  April 1821.
- The inversion is the point: the seat of a European empire moved to its
  own colony. Brazil gained government institutions and the right to
  trade directly with the world — privileges its elites then refused to
  give back.
- **7 September 1822**: Pedro proclaims independence at the Ipiranga.
  **12 October 1822**: he is declared Emperor Pedro I; crowned
  1 December. An empire, not a republic — while Spanish America
  fragmented into republics, Brazil stayed one country under a monarch
  who was heir to the throne it had just left.
- José Bonifácio de Andrada e Silva and other Brazilian advisers pushed
  Pedro to break with Portugal **before separatist movements did it
  without him** — the throughline in a single sentence.
- **There was a war.** Portuguese forces held out in Bahia, Maranhão and
  Pará; Pedro hired Admiral Thomas Cochrane and Pierre Labatut to fight
  them. Portugal recognised independence in the Treaty of Friendship and
  Alliance, **29 August 1825**, roughly three years after the
  declaration.

**Angle.** The court's flight to Rio in 1808, the inversion of colony and
metropole, and 1822 — independence declared by the heir to the Portuguese
throne, producing an empire while Spanish America fragmented into
republics. The clearest single illustration of the throughline.

**Grammar: defining relative clauses.** *The prince who declared…*, *the
decree that moved the capital…* — identifying which of several, which is
what this article constantly needs.

**Care needed.** "Independence without bloodshed" is overstated — there
was fighting in Bahia, Maranhão, Pará and Cisplatina. Say *negotiated*,
not *peaceful*.

### 4. Abolition, and who actually ended slavery

**Sources:**

- [Alonso, *The Last Abolition: The Brazilian Antislavery Movement, 1868–1888* — H-Net review](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=57450) — the key modern account
- [Brazilian Abolitionism, Its Historiography, and the Uses of Political History — *Journal of Latin American Studies*, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-latin-american-studies/article/abs/brazilian-abolitionism-its-historiography-and-the-uses-of-political-history/8507E27145BD3EA440EF8E270B0B0E6D)
- [Upheaval, Violence, and the Abolition of Slavery in Brazil: The Case of São Paulo — *Hispanic American Historical Review*, Duke](https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article/49/4/639/157334/Upheaval-Violence-and-the-Abolition-of-Slavery-in)
- [The decline and fall of slavery in Brazil (1850–88) — University of London Press](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/brazil/section/6edef003-fbde-4bb8-813e-6aa932c8c182)
- [Abolition — *Brazil: Five Centuries of Change*, Brown University Library](https://library.brown.edu/create/fivecenturiesofchange/chapters/chapter-4/abolition/)
- [Quilombos, hotbeds of Afro-Brazilian resistance — *UNESCO Courier*](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/quilombos-hotbeds-afro-brazilian-resistance)
- [Slave and abolitionist (Luiz Gama) — *Revista Pesquisa FAPESP*](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/slave-abolitionist/)

**Facts the article can use:**

- The *Lei Áurea* was signed on **13 May 1888**, freeing roughly
  **700,000** people — the last abolition in the Americas.
- **The decisive pressure was flight, not legislation.** In the two years
  before the law, enslaved people left the coffee plantations of São
  Paulo and Rio in growing numbers — "at first secretly one by one, and
  later in mass and almost publicly." Fugitive networks formed around
  towns such as **Santos** and **Campos**. Revolts accelerated through
  **1887**, some involving large groups from neighbouring *fazendas*.
  By 1888 the institution was collapsing where it mattered most
  economically.
- Quilombos were not only Palmares and not only colonial: collective
  escapes and new quilombos continued **up to the eve of abolition**,
  and frightened planters and authorities throughout the country.
- The three best-known Black abolitionists worked in different arenas,
  which is why the movement reached everywhere at once:
  - **Luiz Gama** — lawyer, himself formerly enslaved and illegally sold
    as a child; won large numbers of freedom suits by stretching the
    legal definition of lawful enslavement to its limit.
  - **José do Patrocínio** — pharmacist turned journalist; the movement's
    public voice and street organiser.
  - **André Rebouças** — engineer and professor; the insider who
    connected the courtrooms to the drawing rooms. He and Patrocínio
    founded the Abolitionist Confederation.
- **The sharpest point in the scholarship**: Alonso argues the "abolition
  was the handiwork of the Crown" story was cultivated *after* 1888 —
  partly by Rebouças and Patrocínio themselves. The myth this article
  dismantles was in part built by the people it credits.

**Angle.** This is the article that argues **against** the series'
throughline. The *Lei Áurea* of 1888 looks like the perfect
transition-from-above — a law signed by a princess. The historiography
says otherwise: mass flight from plantations, quilombos, the abolitionist
movement including Black abolitionists such as Luiz Gama, José do
Patrocínio and André Rebouças, and an institution already collapsing.
The series is stronger for containing its own counter-argument.

**Grammar: nominalisation.** The standout alignment of the series.
Nominalisation is the grammar that hides who did what — *the abolition of
slavery*, *the granting of freedom* — and this article is precisely about
recovering the agents that phrasing erases. Make the connection explicit
in the spotlight.

**Care needed.** The "Princess Isabel freed the slaves" story is a myth
this article exists to dismantle; do not reproduce it uncritically even
as setup. Equally, do not overcorrect into claiming the law was
meaningless — 700,000 people's legal status changed on one day.

Two specific traps found during research:

- **Luiz Gama's mother.** He is often said to be the son of Luíza Mahin,
  linked to the 1835 Malê revolt in Bahia. The connection is traditional
  but not securely documented. Say he was born free to an African mother
  and illegally sold into slavery as a child — which is documented — and
  leave Mahin out unless a solid source is found.
- **Abolition did not end the story.** The law provided no land, no
  compensation and no citizenship guarantees for freed people, and
  "post-abolition" is a whole field in Brazilian historiography. The
  article should gesture at this without turning into a different
  article.

### 5. Vargas and the invention of modern Brazil

**Sources:**

- [Getúlio Vargas — Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Getulio-Vargas)
- [Brazil: The Vargas Era — Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brazil/The-Vargas-era)
- [Vargas, Getúlio — Portal Contemporâneo da América Latina e Caribe, USP](https://sites.usp.br/portalatinoamericano/en/espanol-vargas-getulio) — Brazilian academic source
- [Populism in Brazil — University of London Press](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/brazil/section/9c658279-d7e7-49cb-ab8c-90102186849f)
- [World War II and Brazilian Workers: Populism at the Intersections — *International Review of Social History*, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/world-war-ii-and-brazilian-workers-populism-at-the-intersections-between-national-and-global-histories/BFCED0F82F4518248FA9B25EDE4289E3)

**Facts the article can use:**

- **1930**: Vargas takes power in the Revolution of 1930, ending the Old
  Republic. He would hold power for fifteen years.
- **10 November 1937**: the *Estado Novo* is declared, after a claimed
  communist threat. Openly dictatorial. It ends **29 October 1945**.
- **1943**: the *Consolidação das Leis do Trabalho* (CLT) — a single
  comprehensive labour code. This is the piece of the Vargas era that
  still shapes daily working life in Brazil, which is exactly why the
  period cannot be filed simply under "dictatorship".
- Union rights were legally recognised, wages and welfare extended — but
  within a structure that kept unions under state control. Rights were
  granted, not won, and the state kept the machinery.
- Propaganda built him as *o pai dos pobres*, the father of the poor.
  Critics answered with *mãe dos ricos* — mother of the rich.
- **24 August 1954**: facing a corruption crisis, Vargas shoots himself,
  leaving a letter blaming foreign and domestic interests for the
  campaign against him. The letter transformed his reputation
  immediately and is a primary document worth quoting.

**Angle.** 1930 to 1954: the end of the *café com leite* republic, the
Estado Novo dictatorship, labour legislation that still structures
Brazilian working life, and a suicide letter that reshaped his memory.
Shows the pattern repeating in the twentieth century.

**Grammar: *used to* and *would* for past habits.** How work, cities and
the state used to function, and what changed — the natural grammar for
describing a way of life that ended.

**Care needed.** Vargas is genuinely contested in Brazil: authoritarian
and the author of workers' rights. Present both; do not resolve it.

### 6. Twenty-one years, and the deal that ended them

**Sources:**

- [Comissão Nacional da Verdade — final report, official portal](https://cnv.memoriasreveladas.gov.br/index.php/outros-destaques/574-conheca-e-acesse-o-relatorio-final-da-cnv) — primary
- [CNV final report, Volume 1 (PDF) — gov.br / Memórias Reveladas](https://www.gov.br/memoriasreveladas/pt-br/assuntos/comissoes-da-verdade/volume_1_digital.pdf) — primary
- [CNV delivers final report — Agência Brasil, December 2014](https://agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br/direitos-humanos/noticia/2014-12/comissao-nacional-da-verdade-entrega-relatorio-final-e-encerra)
- [Diretas Já — Google Arts & Culture / Folha de S.Paulo archive](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/direct-elections-now-folha-de-sp/3QXhh2z6SaElnA)

**Facts the article can use:**

- **13 December 1968 — AI-5.** Congress closed, censorship of press and
  the arts, political rights suspended, habeas corpus removed for
  political crimes. The repression peaked between 1968 and 1974.
- **The CNV report** (delivered 10 December 2014, after two years and
  seven months of work; three volumes, over 4,000 pages) named **434
  people** killed or disappeared, and concluded that illegal detention,
  torture, sexual violence, execution and concealment of bodies were the
  result of **state policy**, amounting to crimes against humanity. Use
  this as the citable basis rather than looser estimates.
- **1979 Amnesty Law** — covered both those who resisted and those who
  repressed. This is what makes the transition "negotiated" in the
  strong sense: it protected the outgoing regime.
- **Diretas Já, 1984** — the largest political demonstrations in
  Brazilian history to that point, peaking at around **1.5 million
  people in São Paulo on 16 April 1984**. The Dante de Oliveira
  amendment for direct elections then **fell 22 votes short** of the
  two-thirds needed in the Chamber.
- **15 January 1985** — Tancredo Neves elected by an **electoral
  college**, not by voters: 480 votes to Paulo Maluf's 180. He died on
  **21 April 1985** before taking office; his vice-president José Sarney
  became president.
- The closing irony for the series: the country returned to democracy
  through an indirect election, after a direct-election amendment
  failed, under an amnesty that shielded the regime. Even the end of the
  dictatorship was arranged from above — while the largest street
  mobilisation in the country's history had demanded otherwise.

**Angle.** 1964 to 1985: a coup presented as a brief corrective that
lasted two decades, AI-5, censorship and torture, *Diretas Já*, and a
transition completed by indirect election with an amnesty covering both
sides. Ends the series on the throughline's most uncomfortable case —
even the return to democracy was negotiated from above.

**Grammar: future in the past.** *What was going to be a brief
intervention lasted twenty-one years*; *they said the measures would be
temporary*. The gap between what was promised and what happened is both
the grammar and the subject.

**Care needed.** Findings of the Comissão Nacional da Verdade
(2011–2014) are the citable basis for claims about torture and deaths —
use it rather than looser sources. Keep to the documented record; this is
living memory for the learner's family generation.

## Coherence notes

- **The throughline is a thesis, not a frame.** Article 4 argues against
  it on purpose. Do not "fix" that inconsistency — a series that only
  confirms itself is weaker, and the counter-case is where the strongest
  historiography sits.
- **Ordering is chronological**, because the subjects have a genuine time
  axis and the argument accumulates. No further justification needed.
- **New topic value: `history`.** First article outside
  technology/philosophy. Chosen over `brazilian-history` so future
  non-Brazil history fits the same filter. This becomes a visible value
  in the search page's Topic dropdown.
- **The entire grammar rotation was used up by the previous series** (all
  eleven points). All six below are new: reporting passives, past perfect
  / narrative tenses, defining relative clauses, nominalisation, *used
  to*/*would*, future in the past. Add them to
  `docs/class-structure.md` when the series is approved.
- **Sourcing standard is higher than usual here.** The learner knows this
  subject; errors will be obvious to him in a way they were not for the
  AI series. Prefer Brazilian academic sources and primary records
  (IBGE, Biblioteca Nacional, Slave Voyages, the CNV report) over
  English-language summaries, and check any figure that looks round.
